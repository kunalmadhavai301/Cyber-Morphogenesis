Abstract
Modern enterprise and cyber-physical networks face increasingly sophisticated coordinated multi-agent attacks that exploit dynamic topological shifts, perimeter address translation, and ephemeral communications to evade conventional defenses. Existing graph neural network (GNN) and intrusion detection frameworks rely heavily on static or associational graph representations, rendering them vulnerable to node-degree blindness under Network Address Translation (NAT) and unable to distinguish genuine causal propagation from spurious statistical correlations. In this paper, we propose Cyber Morphogenesis, a unified framework combining dynamic graph representation learning, time-lagged causal discovery, and counterfactual defense under Pearl’s do-calculus. Cyber Morphogenesis models network activity as a time-evolving attributed multigraph, preserving edge-weight concentration and temporal dependencies to overcome identity collapse. We formulate a vector autoregressive causal discovery engine with false discovery rate control to reconstruct directed causal attack chains despite unobserved confounding. Leveraging structural causal models, the framework evaluates counterfactual interventions—such as selective host isolation, link throttling, and dynamic flow timeout adaptation—to optimize threat containment while minimizing operational service disruption. Empirical evaluations against multi-agent attack simulations demonstrate that our framework achieves high causal graph reconstruction fidelity over correlation baselines, maintains robust attack propagation forecasting across multiple prediction horizons ( at ), and reduces the attack success rate from to under counterfactual interventions with only operational service disruption. Furthermore, a controlled collapse-ratio sweep confirms our model’s resilience across varying degrees of network address translation.
Keywords: Dynamic cyber graphs, causal discovery, counterfactual defense, coordinated multi-agent attacks, graph neural networks, network intrusion detection.
I. INTRODUCTION AND LITERATURE REVIEW
A. Research Background and Problem Statement
Modern cyber environments—spanning cloud enterprise platforms, Software-Defined Networks (SDN), the Internet of Things (IoT), and Industrial Control Systems (ICS)—have undergone profound structural transformations. In these environments, adversarial threats are no longer isolated, single-step script executions; instead, they manifest as coordinated, multi-stage, and multi-agent attack campaigns. Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) botnets, advanced persistent threat (APT) lateral movements, and coordinated deception attacks operate across multiple hosts and protocol layers, intentionally camouflaging malicious traffic within massive volumes of legitimate network background noise.
To model the relational dependencies of network traffic, graph-based intrusion detection systems (NIDS) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as the leading analytical paradigm. Wang et al. [1] proposed a behavior similarity-based graph attention network (BS-GAT) to overcome uneven graph construction and overfitting in edge computing. However, graph-based NIDS almost universally rely on node-centric structural features—such as in-degree, out-degree, and neighborhood aggregations—which implicitly assume that every attacker is visible as a distinct node in the host-communication topology.
As critically revealed by Hasibuzzaman [2], this fundamental visibility assumption collapses under common network infrastructure mechanisms such as Network Address Translation (NAT), load balancers, and perimeter aggregation gateways. When multiple attacking agents operate behind a NAT gateway, their individual IP identities are rewritten to a single gateway address. Consequently, node-degree features become mathematically blind to true attack volume, causing standard GNNs to experience severe detection failures [2].
Furthermore, while spatiotemporal graph learning models such as ST-GAT-Fusion by Wang et al. [3] and fault-tolerant temporal architectures by Yao and Koirala [4] capture structural and sequential patterns, they fundamentally rely on statistical associations rather than causal relationships. In dynamic cyber systems, statistical correlation does not equal causation: high-volume benign bursts, shared upstream routing, and multi-protocol synchronization frequently induce spurious correlations that mimic attack propagation. In enterprise audit logs, provenance graphs can balloon into tens of millions of nodes and edges; frameworks like CSCProv by An et al. [5], knowledge-graph models by Ren et al. [6], and process-centric encoders like ProcSAGE by Xu et al. [7] seek to reduce provenance complexity. Yet, when defending against coordinated attacks, existing systems lack the capability to answer foundational counterfactual questions: “What would have been the probability of system compromise if host had been isolated, or if link had been throttled at time ?”
Current dynamic and zero-day defense models—such as the continuous-coordinate implicit neural representation in GraphINR-IDS by Deepanramkumar and Sharmila [8], the Mahalanobis-Weibull open-set framework MaGOS-IDS by Nguyen and Park [9], and stochastic attacker–defender game formulations by Makarem et al. [10]—tackle open-set detection or strategic interaction. Nevertheless, they lack an integrated causal inference mechanism to evaluate proactive intervention policies and isolate the operational costs of defensive actions.

The landscape of graph learning, attack modeling, and dynamic defense encompasses several distinct research threads:
Graph Neural Networks and Topology Robustness: BS-GAT [1] introduces behavioral similarity rules to balance neighbor degree distributions in edge networks. [3] combine spatial GAT with Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) for situation awareness. Yao and Koirala [4] present DTGNN-FIDS, introducing fault-modulated attention layers and -fault consistency loss for industrial 5G networks. Chatterjee [15] proposes proximal-gradient adversarial training for GNNs under DDoS perturbations, while Isaac et al. [17] introduce EdgeFence for federated temporal GNN malware detection.
Host Provenance and Knowledge Graphs: Ren et al. [6] construct knowledge graphs linking CVE, CWE, and CAPEC for GCN-based APT detection. Xu et al. [7] design ProcSAGE, focusing graph learning strictly on process and thread nodes to reduce memory by 78%. An et al. [5] develop CSCProv, enforcing causal-semantic consistency between GCN structural embeddings and SecBERT language representations. Liu et al. [12] propose APT-HERA, learning node representations from dual homophily and heterogeneity views.
Zero-Day Detection, Open-Set Recognition, and Self-Supervision: GraphINR-IDS [8] formulates benign traffic modeling as a continuous coordinate field via SIREN and Laplacian eigenvectors. MaGOS-IDS [9] couples edge-aware GCN with regularized Mahalanobis distance and Extreme Value Theory (EVT) to establish calibrated open-set reject boundaries. Zhao et al. [20] propose GDAE, utilizing dual autoencoders (local structure and global semantics) with Kendall uncertainty weighting and EVT thresholding. Miryahyaei et al. [21] introduce focal causal temporal CNNs (FCTCNNs) to classify rare, imbalanced attacks hierarchically.
Attack Graphs, Strategic Games, and Security Twins: Ibrahim et al. [11] employ AADL, AGREE, and JKind model checking to generate formal attack graphs for cyber-physical systems. Unger et al. [13] extend attack graphs with feasibility metrics and countermeasure nodes aligned with ISO/SAE 21434 and CLC/TS 50701. Makarem et al. [10] formulate a repeated zero-sum stochastic game over an attack graph, comparing Q-learning and DQN defenders against learning attackers. Okutan and Yang [16] introduce ASSERT, using dynamic Bayesian classifiers and DBSCAN to separate streaming attack models. Baiardi and Sammartino [19] develop NotLine, building a non-intrusive Security Twin via hypoexponential discovery and Monte Carlo adversary simulations. Prasad and Sharma [22] develop AGEIN and SA-GEN for Digital Twin-SDN security, utilizing Shannon entropy and Deep Q-Learning Markov Decision Processes (MDP) to adapt flow timeouts.

C. Identification of the Research Gap
A rigorous examination of the literature reveals three fundamental, unaddressed gaps:
The Identity-Collapse vs. Causal Topology Gap: Existing graph-based detection systems rely on node degrees and static adjacency matrices [1], [3], [12]. While Hasibuzzaman [2] established that edge weight concentration rescues detection under NAT perimeter collapse, edge weight alone merely confirms the presence of anomalous traffic; it cannot reconstruct the multi-hop, directed causal propagation path connecting distributed attacking agents to critical internal victims.
The Correlation vs. Causal Discovery Gap: Deep graph representations and temporal sequence models [4], [7], [14], [20], [21] measure statistical co-occurrence. In coordinated multi-agent campaigns—where attackers intentionally execute simultaneous volumetric floods to mask stealthy reconnaissance and exfiltration [10], [16], [22]—correlational models infer spurious edges between uncoordinated hosts, generating debilitating false positives and misleading forensic reconstructions.
The Observational vs. Counterfactual Defense Gap: All reviewed defensive systems [1], [4], [9], [13], [18], [19], [22] are strictly observational. They either classify observed events or trigger pre-scripted mitigation policies (e.g., blanket port blocking or static access revocation). No existing cybersecurity framework applies Pearlian counterfactual reasoning () to rigorously evaluate what-if defensive interventions in dynamic graphs, balancing guaranteed threat containment against the collateral operational cost of service disruption.
D. Research Motivation, Objectives, and Research Questions
Motivated by these critical shortcomings, this paper investigates the hypothesis: Can a dynamic causal graph framework, combined with counterfactual intervention reasoning, improve the detection, propagation prediction, and adaptive containment of coordinated multi-agent cyberattacks in dynamic systems?
To investigate this, we formulate four concrete Research Questions (RQs):
RQ1 (Dynamic Causal Discovery): Can time-lagged conditional independence and edge-weight concentration reconstruct the true causal DAG of multi-agent attacks under identity collapse and background noise, outperforming correlation-based graphs?
RQ2 (Attack Propagation Forecasting): Does conditioning on an evolving causal graph improve multi-step attack progression prediction across extended temporal horizons compared to static GCN, LSTM, and random walk baselines?
RQ3 (Counterfactual Intervention Evaluation): How effectively can Pearl’s -calculus evaluate alternative defensive interventions (host isolation, path throttling, timeout adaptation) to minimize the Attack Success Rate (ASR) while constraining the Service Disruption Rate (SDR)?
RQ4 (Adversarial Robustness and Adaptation): Does the proposed framework maintain its defensive efficacy when coordinated attackers adaptively alter their strategies in a stochastic arms race?
E. Contributions of this Paper
The key contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
The Cyber Morphogenesis Conceptual Framework: We introduce Cyber Morphogenesis, a formal framework that models dynamic network topologies, multi-agent adversarial interactions, and defensive adaptations as continuous structural causal transformations.
Causal Graph Discovery under Identity Collapse: We formulate an edge-weight-concentrated, time-lagged vector autoregressive causal discovery algorithm that explicitly controls for latent confounding and false discovery rates, resolving the node-degree blindness identified in [2].
Counterfactual Defense Engine via Pearlian do-Calculus: We develop a formal counterfactual evaluation mechanism that simulates hypothetical defensive actions on the structural causal model (SCM), providing principled trade-off frontiers between threat containment and operational availability.
Empirical Validation across Benchmarks and Multi-Agent Scenarios: We implement the complete framework and execute reproducible experimental simulations, measuring actual precision, recall, Structural Hamming Distance (SHD), attack propagation horizons, and counterfactual containment metrics.

C. Coordinated Multi-Agent Attack Dynamics
We model adversarial campaigns as a repeated stochastic multi-agent game [10] with three specialized malicious agent roles:
Agent 1 (Reconnaissance & Probing): Scans exposed ports and extracts service information from edge gateways, inducing subtle changes in destination port entropy [18].
Agent 2 (Exploitation & Lateral Movement): Infiltrates vulnerable services, exploits host software weaknesses (mapped from CVE/CWE databases [6], [13]), establishes command-and-control (C2) persistence, and pivots across intermediate nodes toward target databases.
Agent 3 (Coordinated Volumetric Disruption): Launches synchronized high-volume flood attacks (DDoS) designed to overwhelm firewalls or trigger failover modes [2], [4], [15], masking Agent 2's lateral movements.
The overall state transition is non-stationary: agents coordinate by timing their actions to coincide with maximum defender uncertainty.

G. Critical Discussion of Findings
Association versus Causation in Cybersecurity: The findings demonstrate that high correlation between network events is insufficient to justify defensive intervention. Misidentifying a correlated but non-causal host (such as an innocent internal DNS server or logging host) as an attack vector leads to self-inflicted denial-of-service when that host is isolated. Causal discovery provides the necessary mathematical filter.
Operational Trade-offs in Counterfactual Defense: Defense cannot be evaluated in a vacuum of 100% security. As demonstrated in Table IV, aggressive isolation of internal administrators () incurs a prohibitive operational disruption without preventing external DMZ compromise. The Pareto-optimal strategy lies in choking causal bottleneck edges rather than isolating end assets.
Adversarial Co-Adaptation: In multi-agent games, attackers continuously probe and pivot. As established in [10], stationary defense assumptions severely underestimate vulnerability. The Cyber Morphogenesis framework's adaptive feedback loop dynamically updates the SCM as new edge observations arrive, ensuring that counterfactual recommendations remain calibrated under non-stationary conditions.
IV. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
A. Summary of Verified Findings
This paper introduced Cyber Morphogenesis, a unified framework for causal discovery and counterfactual defense against coordinated multi-agent cyberattacks in dynamic networks. By integrating edge-weight concentration, time-lagged conditional independence testing, and structural causal modeling, the framework resolves the fundamental vulnerability of node-degree blindness under perimeter address translation. Robustness against the full spectrum of NAT collapse ratios () where classical metrics fail.
B. Limitations
We explicitly acknowledge four key limitations:
Causal Sufficiency Assumption: The framework assumes that all critical intermediate communications pass through observable network telemetry vantage points. Unmonitored out-of-band communication channels (e.g., direct physical USB transfers or unmonitored encrypted side-channels) introduce latent confounding that can degrade causal graph estimation.
Stationarity within Estimation Windows: The time-lagged vector autoregressive tests assume local stationarity across the immediate sliding observation window. Extremely abrupt, sub-second tactical shifts by adversaries may require finer micro-batching.
Single-Domain Emulation: While the evaluated topology captures DMZ, corporate, and OT subnets, extensive cross-organization validation on live production clouds with tens of thousands of hosts remains to be conducted.
Computational Complexity of Causal Search: Pairwise conditional independence testing scales quadratically with network vertex count (), requiring hierarchical subnet partitioning for hyperscale deployment.
C. Future Research Directions
Future work will focus on:
Continuous-Time Neural Causal SCMs: Integrating neural ordinary differential equations (Neural ODEs) and continuous-time graph embeddings to model causal attack flows without discrete time-windowing.
Decentralized Multi-Agent Counterfactual Reinforcement Learning: Developing distributed, federated multi-agent defense agents that learn cooperative counterfactual containment policies across zero-trust cloud domains.
Empirical Validation in Operational Cyber Ranges: Deploying the framework within large-scale live cyber ranges featuring active human red teams to quantify resilience against novel zero-day exploits and adversarial evasion.
